**Eight fresh candidates**

These are newly synthesized proposals, not claims that their categories have no prior art. Evidence establishes the underlying work or difficulty; it does not establish demand for these exact products.

**1. Take Two — a production room that rescues tomorrow’s shoot**  
*Track 02; potentially Track 01 for small production teams.*

**Who suffers:** student filmmakers, independent crews and small commercial teams. Losing a location or cast window can invalidate several departments’ plans simultaneously. ScreenSkills describes assistant directors coordinating cast, locations, equipment and departmental schedules, including creative responses to unexpected changes. That verifies a coordination-heavy job; a monetary estimate of disruption for our target users is **not verified**. [ScreenSkills](https://www.screenskills.com/job-profiles/browse/film-and-tv-drama/production-management/assistant-director).

**Switch necessity:** a director, scheduling agent and continuity agent share the script and constraints in a production room. Exclusive roles establish current responsibility; documents preserve revisions; a native question captures the creative compromise. A linked crew room receives only the approved call sheet.

**Remembered moment:** “We lost the rooftop.” A pretty alternative fails the availability check; the director chooses a feasible alternative that preserves the scene’s secrecy.

**Risk and overlap:** medium risk if bounded to three scenes and two locations. Existing production software is strong competition; no direct hackathon overlap found. Do not build general film scheduling.

**2. Heirloom — an oral-history studio where the narrator controls publication**  
*Track 02.*

**Who suffers:** families and community historians producing stories from interviews, photographs and conflicting recollections. An attractive generated biography can misrepresent someone or publish material they wanted withheld. The Oral History Association explicitly recommends documenting informed consent and, where possible, allowing narrator approval before public release. Severity is potentially irreversible disclosure or misrepresentation; prevalence for our proposed users is **not verified**. [OHA best practices](https://oralhistory.org/best-practices/).

**Switch necessity:** interview, editorial and publication rooms separate purposes and memberships. Researcher and editor agents work from attached source documents; the narrator answers questions about disputed wording and release scope. A custom publisher exports only approved passages.

**Remembered moment:** the narrator says, “That happened, but it is not for the public version.” The illustrated story removes that passage while preserving the private source and decision.

**Risk and overlap:** medium. Use supplied transcripts rather than building transcription, voice cloning or preservation infrastructure. No direct observed overlap, although generic memoir generators are obvious substitutes. Adoption is difficult if relatives do not use a supported chat app.

**3. Subtitle Relay — negotiate meaning, timing and accessibility together**  
*Track 02.*

**Who suffers:** volunteer translation teams and small creators localizing videos. Translators must preserve meaning within measurable reading constraints. TED specifies limits including two subtitle lines and a maximum reading speed of 21 characters per second. These are concrete editorial constraints, not evidence of a quantified market opportunity. [TED subtitling guidance](https://www.ted.com/participate/translate/subtitling-tips).

**Switch necessity:** one source room links to language rooms. Translator and reviewer roles exchange proposals against a shared glossary; a human creator decides when a joke or cultural reference should change. Approved versions return explicitly to the release room.

**Remembered moment:** a literal translation fits the dictionary but loses the joke; the replacement preserves it and passes the timing checker in a synchronized video preview.

**Risk and overlap:** low to medium. Support one short video and two languages, starting from supplied subtitles. Export WebVTT; avoid dubbing. No direct observed entry, but translation and subtitle editors are crowded markets. Switch’s contribution is collaborative editorial resolution, not translation itself.

**4. Cover Circle — close the gaps in a family’s care rota**  
*Track 02.*

**Who suffers:** unpaid carers coordinating meals, transport, visits and respite. Carers UK reports that **57% of respondents** to its State of Caring 2024 survey felt overwhelmed often or always. That demonstrates serious burden, though it does not isolate scheduling as the cause or prove this workflow reduces it. [Carers UK](https://www.carersuk.org/reports/state-of-caring-the-impact-of-caring-on-carers-mental-health-and-the-need-for-support-from-social-care-services).

**Switch necessity:** a family room holds agreed commitments; a restricted coordination room holds relevant details. Agents identify uncovered tasks and propose swaps, but people explicitly accept responsibilities through native questions. Room history distinguishes a suggestion from a commitment.

**Remembered moment:** “I cannot do Thursday.” Several plausible swaps still leave the return journey uncovered; the system exposes that gap before anyone assumes the day is covered.

**Risk and overlap:** medium-high because correctness, privacy and adoption matter. Limit the MVP to nonclinical logistics, with no medication or medical recommendations. No direct observed overlap. Discord or Mattermost may be a poor fit for many families, weakening the product despite strong need.

**5. Open Doors — turn accessibility requests into owned event commitments**  
*Track 01 for organizers; Track 02 for community events.*

**Who suffers:** disabled attendees and small event teams whose accessibility information is scattered across venue, speaker and volunteer conversations. W3C’s event guidance spans accessible spaces, participant requirements and presentation arrangements. This establishes that accessibility crosses organizational boundaries; it does not establish that software can certify a venue. [W3C event checklist](https://www.w3.org/WAI/teach-advocate/accessible-presentations/).

**Switch necessity:** venue, programme and organizer rooms have different owners. Agents assemble an access plan from documented answers, route unresolved questions across linked rooms and record human commitments. Sensitive requests stay within explicitly chosen memberships.

**Remembered moment:** the accessible entrance exists, but the proposed room change makes the stage unreachable. The attendee identifies the requirement; the organizer changes the room and volunteer instructions together.

**Risk and overlap:** medium. Build around one fictional event and explicit venue facts. Label unverified arrangements clearly. No direct observed overlap. The danger is presenting checklist completion as accessibility assurance; the product must expose unresolved requirements rather than announce compliance.

**6. Change Order — make a renovation change understandable before agreeing**  
*Track 01.*

**Who suffers:** homeowners, designers and small contractors coordinating alterations through informal conversations. A seemingly small material change can affect quantities, delivery dates and subsequent trades. RICS advises agreeing how changes are handled and their time and cost implications. The financial consequences can be substantial, but a representative loss figure is **not verified**. [RICS homeowner guidance](https://www.rics.org/consumer-guides/home-extensions-improvements).

**Switch necessity:** customer and contractor rooms share an approved specification through explicit snapshots. Estimator and scheduling roles compare proposals; both humans review the same version before the coordinator records agreement.

**Remembered moment:** “These tiles are cheaper.” The material saving is outweighed by an extra installation visit and delayed plumbing; both sides see the complete change.

**Risk and overlap:** medium. Use structured quotes and arithmetic, not drawing interpretation or legal-contract generation. No direct observed overlap; ClearSend is adjacent through approvals and money. The larger weakness is channel adoption: many intended customers already coordinate through WhatsApp, whose suitability here is **not verified**.

**7. Grant Shift — reconcile a charity’s changed plan with its funding**  
*Track 01.*

**Who suffers:** small nonprofits whose programme, finance and fundraising staff maintain different versions of a project. NCVO explains that restricted funding must support the agreed activity and that unexpected circumstances may require a budget change. This supports a consequential coordination problem without proving demand for an agent product. [NCVO budget guidance](https://www.ncvo.org.uk/help-and-guidance/funding-income/all-about-grants/preparing-your-budget/).

**Switch necessity:** programme and finance rooms hold different source documents. Agents reconcile activities, quantities and budgets; a human resolves the trade-off. The approved amendment is recorded before a funder-facing draft is produced.

**Remembered moment:** the narrative promises 120 workshops while the revised budget funds 80. The mismatch becomes a decision, not a polished but contradictory application.

**Risk and overlap:** medium-low with structured fixtures. Avoid promising funding eligibility or accounting compliance. No direct observed overlap; evidence-led writing overlaps somewhat with IDEAForge’s presentation. It is practical and technically convincing, but less visually distinctive than Take Two and harder to explain emotionally in two minutes.

**8. Canon Keeper — a tabletop campaign room that remembers without overruling players**  
*Track 02.*

**Who suffers:** game masters and players maintaining campaign facts, character decisions and secrets between sessions. Practitioner Mike Shea’s preparation method explicitly organizes characters, scenes, secrets, locations and NPCs into campaign notes. This verifies the information burden; its severity is recreational frustration and preparation time rather than economic harm. [Sly Flourish](https://slyflourish.com/organizing_notes.html).

**Switch necessity:** public party and private GM rooms have genuinely different knowledge. Chronicler and continuity roles propose updates; players confirm their characters’ consequential choices. A custom arbiter publishes only approved canon.

**Remembered moment:** the recap says a character accepted a bargain. The player disputes it; the cited session passage supports them; the public campaign record changes without exposing the GM’s secret.

**Risk and overlap:** medium. Build a between-session workflow, not a complete game engine, live transcription service or autonomous GM. No direct observed hackathon overlap, but AI-GM and campaign-note products are plentiful. The central challenge is demonstrating useful collaboration rather than entertaining agent dialogue.

The maintainer story also needs nuance: Daniel Stenberg’s October post says AI-assisted reports can identify real vulnerabilities and be accurate. Revised Slop Court accommodates that, but the name and opening explanation must overcome their own framing. [Stenberg’s account](https://daniel.haxx.se/blog/2026/10/22/ignore-the-slop/).

Take Two replaces untrusted-code execution and GitHub mutations with structured proposals, deterministic checks and a local artifact. That is a smaller correctness surface. It also gives this builder a natural visual language: script pages, scene cards, a shooting timeline and an animatic.

Crucially, **humans contribute something agents cannot derive from constraints: which artistic compromise is acceptable**. The director is doing the work, not merely approving the agents’ answer.

**Champion pitch — 150 words**

Tomorrow’s short film has three scenes, two actors, and one rooftop. Then the location owner cancels. Your crew already has yesterday’s call sheet. Take Two turns the crew’s chat into a production room where humans and specialist agents rescue the shoot together. A scheduling agent checks availability and travel; a continuity agent protects the story’s essential beats. They propose different compromises, with every change tied to the script and constraints. The director chooses what must survive through a native Switch question. A deterministic checker rejects impossible schedules before anyone receives a revised call sheet. Only the approved version reaches the crew room, with a clear change list and acknowledgments. Switch holds the shared brief, routes work to live roles, records the decision, and connects the people doing the shoot. The demo ends with a feasible shooting plan and a playable storyboard showing the director’s chosen compromise, ready for the crew.

**Core architecture**

Build **one active film, two reasoning agents, one deterministic coordinator and two rooms**.

The production room contains the director, coordinator, scheduling agent and continuity agent. Its documents hold the script, declared constraints, candidate plans and approved revision. The crew room contains the approved call sheet and changes, without the production deliberation.

The coordinator is a small custom protocol agent. It creates mutable canonical documents itself, delegates through Switch, validates returned structured proposals and opens the director’s question. SQLite stores delivery IDs, revision identifiers and retry state; Switch holds the collaboration record.

The validator checks cast availability, location windows, overlapping bookings and declared travel durations. Literary continuity findings remain **agent concerns for human judgment**, not mathematically proven errors.

A local visual board projects the Switch-backed state. It is not an alternative orchestration channel. Workers receive their assignments and shared inputs through Switch; the director’s decision returns through Switch.

The coordinator publishes the approved projection explicitly to the crew room. Receipt confirmation is an application workflow using authenticated human replies—not a claim that Switch automatically supplies call-sheet acknowledgments.

**The demo and the 3.5-day boundary**

Use a tiny Bengaluru short about a promise, a confrontation and a reconciliation. Establish the script and initial feasible schedule before introducing the disruption.

1. The director reports that rooftop permission has been withdrawn.
2. The timeline marks the affected scene and dependencies.
3. Scheduler proposes alternative locations; continuity explains how a public café changes the private confrontation.
4. The director chooses the compromise through the native question.
5. The validator checks the resulting schedule. The crew receives revision two with an explicit change list.
6. A playable storyboard shows how the chosen scene still works.

Demonstrate stale-answer rejection separately: change an availability constraint while a question is pending; its old answer cannot publish an outdated schedule.

Reserve roughly day one for the protocol/room/question path, day two for proposals and validation, day three for the visual board and reproducibility, and the final half-day for recording and documentation. Cut calendar integrations, live weather, automatic script extraction, image generation and a second bridge first.

Windows setup is a real risk: [open issue #1335](https://github.com/sandbox-quantum/switch/issues/1335) reports local PostgreSQL initialization failure. Prove the Docker-based path early. Keep the question prominent because [#1089](https://github.com/sandbox-quantum/switch/issues/1089) concerns discovering pending requests across threads.

Reproducibility should include supplied fixtures, pinned versions, reset instructions and meaningful checks for schedule conflicts, expired decisions and duplicate delivery. An offline replay may illustrate behavior, but must be clearly distinguished from the live Switch demonstration.

Use Wispr throughout the build and capture its Insights scorecard; a voice-led product demo alone does not establish that the project was built by voice. [Side-track requirements](https://www.wemakedevs.org/hackathons/switch).

**Claude’s three strongest attacks**

**1. “This is StudioBinder with agents attached.”**

That objection is substantially correct at the category level. StudioBinder already supports call-sheet collaboration, approvals and confirmation tracking. We must not claim those as novel. [StudioBinder’s own workflow](https://www.studiobinder.com/blog/how-call-sheets-are-approved-on-features-and-television/).

The proposed distinction is **negotiating a disruption across creative intent and practical constraints inside the crew’s conversation**. The call sheet is the resulting artifact. If the demonstration merely generates a schedule, Claude wins this objection. It must show two defensible compromises, a consequential human choice and the resulting coordinated revision.

**2. “Switch is replaceable by a bot and a database.”**

Yes, theoretically. Slop Court is replaceable by GitHub Actions, a controller and a bot too. The fair test is whether Switch performs the submitted system’s essential collaboration.

Here, agent assignments, shared input documents, current role holders, human decisions and approved handoff all pass through Switch. Removing it stops that workflow. A normal bot would require rebuilding those services. Conversely, if the frontend talks privately to models and posts summaries afterward, the Switch score should collapse. The architecture must resist that shortcut.

**3. “You abandoned a prepared project for filmmaking scope creep.”**

This is the strongest execution objection. The answer is a strict boundary: supplied three-scene script, two workers, fixed constraints, simple validator, two rooms and one visual result. No production suite, autonomous directing or generated movie.

The existing Slop Court work inspected here is predominantly planning; replacement sacrifices useful thought, not a running implementation. Its coordinator, revision checking and human-decision concepts transfer.

My recommendation reverses if the new build cannot complete one real **room → agents → question → validated publication** path on day one. But given equal execution discipline, **Take Two gives this particular builder the better chance to be remembered—and a cleaner path to proving that the work actually runs.**
